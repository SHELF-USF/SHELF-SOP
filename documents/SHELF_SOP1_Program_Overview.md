# SHELF SOP 1: Program Overview

## 1.1 Purpose of this SOP

This is the program overview Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) for SHELF.
It introduces the program and explains how the three SOPs that cover
specific operational areas fit together (Section 1.7).

It is written for anyone involved in SHELF. It is especially useful for
onboarding new personnel and serves as an ongoing reference for current team
members. It covers where SHELF came from, what it has learned in each phase,
how it is organized, how to report injuries, how the team uses its shared
tools (OneDrive and GitHub), and how the other SOPs fit beneath it.

---

## 1.2 Program history and motivation

SHELF was funded in response to the Florida RESTORE Act Centers of
Excellence Program (FLRACEP) Request for Proposals 2 (RFP-2). The goal of
RFP-2 was to set up a long-term ecosystem monitoring program in the
Gulf of Mexico (GOM). SHELF was the
study selected to meet that goal.

### SHELF Phase I (2017–2018): Pilot phase

Phase I was a two-year pilot. The SHELF team tested a range of techniques and
methods to find the most effective approach for long-term monitoring:

- The Daily Egg Production Method (DEPM), which used DNA barcoding to
  identify collected eggs to species. This set up the molecular
  identification approach that became central to SHELF's later work.
- Acoustic surveys
- Stable isotope analyses
- Benthic habitat mapping

#### Key findings

- The DEPM estimated the spawning stock biomass (SSB) of vermilion snapper.
  Its estimates were consistent with those from traditional stock assessments
  run through SEDAR (SouthEast Data, Assessment, and Review). This showed that
  egg-based methods could produce fishery-relevant, management-grade data
  products.
- The team quantified the spatial distribution of fish eggs on the northern
  West Florida Shelf (WFS). Using physical oceanography models, it also
  estimated egg retention and export dynamics on the shelf. This gave an early
  framework for understanding how physical oceanography affects the transport of spawned eggs in the
  region.

At the end of Phase I, the FLRACEP Program Management Team and the SHELF team
agreed to focus the program on egg monitoring from then on.

### SHELF Phase II (2019–2022): Establishing the long-term time series

Phase II began just as the COVID-19 pandemic started. Sending field crews out
on research vessels would have meant personnel working and living in close
quarters for long periods. Instead, SHELF shifted its main collection
strategy to a collaboration with SEAMAP (Southeast Area Monitoring and
Assessment Program), with Glenn Zapfe (NOAA) as the main point of contact.

SEAMAP has collected fish eggs in the U.S. Gulf of Mexico with a Continuous
Underway Fish Egg Sampler (CUFES; Checkley et al. 1997) since 2008. CUFES is
also used by the California Cooperative Oceanic Fisheries Investigations (CalCOFI)
and by other labs and programs globally. SEAMAP samples from 2008 through 2011 were completely consumed and
are not available, but archived samples are available from 2012 onward.
SEAMAP could not, however, identify the eggs to species. Most fish eggs look alike across
species, so reliable identification requires DNA barcoding of the
mitochondrial COI gene. The partnership combined SEAMAP's existing egg
collection with SHELF's DNA barcoding capability.

SEAMAP concentrates its main annual collection in August–September (fall).
This is when several economically and ecologically important species spawn,
including red snapper and other snappers (lutjanids), several grouper
species, and mackerels.

#### Key findings

- SHELF built the first three years of what became the long-term fall time
  series, using SEAMAP samples from 2013, 2014, and 2019. The distribution of
  identified eggs across the shelf matched what known species distributions
  predicted. Inshore samples held inshore species, midshelf samples held
  midshelf species, and outer-shelf samples held outer-shelf species. The
  data also showed widespread species present across zones. Because egg distributions matched
  expected adult habitats, this strongly supported the
  idea that egg sampling captures real, biologically and ecologically meaningful spawning
  patterns.
- SHELF also tested metabarcoding (identifying all eggs in a sample together
  rather than one at a time) to cut costs and process more samples.
  Metabarcoding was less expensive and faster, but it performed worse than
  individual egg barcoding in several important ways:

| | Individual barcoding | Metabarcoding |
|---|---|---|
| False positives/negatives | None observed | Substantial |
| Data type | Fully quantitative | Semi-quantitative (presence/absence only) |
| Sample fate | Preserved | Destroyed |

Based on these results, SHELF kept individual egg barcoding as its main
identification method.

### SHELF Phase III (2023–2027): Current phase

Phase III runs from February 1, 2023 through July 31, 2027. It extends the long-term fall time series through 2025 and adds:

- seasonal and habitat-specific sampling (shelf and reef collections in all
  seasons)
- a depth-stratified MOCNESS comparison study
- an evaluation of barcoding success rates using multiple primers

See SHELF SOP 4 (Data Management), Section 4.1.2, for the full list of study
components.

### SHELF Phases IV and V

Later phases will be added to this document as they are funded (see
Section 1.4).

## 1.3 Scientific rationale and management relevance

SHELF's founding goal is to build a long-term time series of fish spawning
data from the annual fall collections. The time series serves two
complementary objectives:

1. **Fisheries management**: SHELF's data products, especially abundance
   indices derived from egg counts, can be used directly in stock
   assessments for managed species. This makes SHELF directly relevant to the
   Gulf of Mexico Fishery Management Council and to SEDAR assessments. SHELF
   also aims to support management at the state level, including the
   Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission (FWC).

2. **Ecosystem-level understanding**: SHELF identifies eggs from many taxa,
   not just managed species. This allows it to study wider ecosystem
   patterns: which species are spawning, where and when reproduction
   happens, and how environmental events such as hurricanes affect spawning
   across the WFS.

Fish eggs make this possible because they hatch quickly. In the Gulf, most
fish eggs hatch into early larvae within about 36–48 hours of spawning, so
eggs are usually collected close to where they were spawned. Larvae, in
contrast, may drift for days to weeks before they are collected. Egg
distributions therefore mark spawning locations far more directly than
larval distributions.

### Extended absences and departures

Key personnel (PIs, lab managers, and graduate students) must notify the PI
in writing at least 30 days before any absence longer than 30 days, and at
least 30 days before leaving the program. Examples include a summer
appointment elsewhere, extended fieldwork for another project, and extended
leave.

Before the absence or departure begins, the person meets with the PI to
hand off their work:

- the status of samples and analyses in progress, and where the samples
  are stored
- all data uploaded to `01_Data/02_tables/staging/` and all working files
  saved in the shared folder (Section 1.8.2), not on a personal computer
- open GitHub issues reassigned to someone else (Section 1.8.3)
- who covers each of their responsibilities during the absence
- whether, and how, they can be reached for urgent questions during the
  absence

When an absence cannot be planned (for example, a medical emergency), notify
the PI as soon as possible. Shorter absences should still be shared with the
PI and the relevant lab manager in advance.

## 1.6 Safety and injury reporting

*Valid as of February 5, 2026. The PI or lab manager confirms the contacts
below at least once a year and before each cruise season.*

This section describes how to report an injury during any SHELF work,
whether in the lab or the field/at sea. It
covers reporting only. It does not decide whether someone is eligible for
workers' compensation benefits, and it does not replace guidance from the
insurance provider or USF Human Resources.

### 1.6.1 Immediately after any injury

1. **Tell your supervisor or the person in charge right away** (the lab
   manager in the lab, the chief scientist or a crew member at sea) so
   that an initial injury report can be completed.
2. **If the injury is life-threatening**, call 911 immediately, or at sea, the U.S. Coast Guard (VHF Channel 16; Sector
   St. Petersburg 24-hour command center, 866-881-1392). At sea, the captain
   or a crew member normally makes this call. Follow all instructions from emergency
   responders.

### 1.6.2 Injuries that are not life-threatening

1. **Injured employee notifies the supervisor (PI).** Do this by email or
   other written documentation as soon as possible after the incident.
2. **Supervisor reports the injury to the insurance provider.** The
   supervisor reports the accident to Amerisys as completely as possible.
   Amerisys runs USF's workers' compensation program and will advise on
   medical treatment and next steps.
3. **Supervisor completes the USF Accident Report**, with input from the
   injured employee. Complete it within 24 hours of the accident whenever
   possible, or as soon as possible if circumstances make 24 hours
   impossible. **Graduate students are classified as "Employee" on this
   form.**
4. **Injured employee follows up with the insurance provider.** After the
   injury is reported, Amerisys will ask the injured employee to call and
   confirm what happened and the nature and extent of the injury. The
   employee is then assigned:
   - a claim number
   - a nurse case manager, who follows up by email at the address given to
     the insurance company

## 1.8 Shared tools: OneDrive and GitHub

### 1.8.1 What is expected

SHELF runs on two shared tools: the **shared OneDrive folder**, where all
data, documents, and the database are kept, and **GitHub**, where the SOPs
and the database scripts are kept and where changes to them are proposed
and tracked. Every SHELF participant is expected to use both, as described
below. This applies to PIs, lab managers, graduate students, and undergraduates.

These tools only work if everyone uses them. When one person keeps files on
a personal drive, sends data or corrections by email or text message, or
edits a private copy of an SOP, the rest of the team works from out-of-date
information, and there is no record of what changed, when, or why. Partial
participation weakens the workflow for the whole program, not just for the
person who opts out.

The table below lists what each person needs to do. Most people need only a
web browser. No one outside the database team needs to use git, the command
line, or RStudio's git tools.

| Task | Who | Tool |
|------|-----|------|
| Store, open, and submit data and documents | Everyone | OneDrive |
| Report a problem or suggest a change to an SOP | Everyone | GitHub, web browser (Section 1.8.4) |
| Read and reply to discussion on issues assigned to you | Everyone | GitHub, web browser or email |
| Commit database scripts | PI, PI's lab manager, anyone writing database scripts | GitHub (SOP 4, Section 4.3.4) |
| Edit the SOP files directly | SOP maintainers (Section 1.8.5) | GitHub |

### 1.8.2 OneDrive shared folder

The shared folder is `1. SHELF_SHARED/`, owned by the PI. Its structure and
naming rules are in SOP 4, Section 4.2.

#### Getting access

1. The PI's lab manager shares `1. SHELF_SHARED/` with your USF account.
   You receive an email invitation.
2. Open the invitation, or go to OneDrive on the web (sign in with your USF
   account) and open **Shared**.
3. Right-click `1. SHELF_SHARED` and choose **Add shortcut to My files**.
4. On your computer, sign in to the OneDrive app with your USF account. The
   folder then appears in File Explorer (Windows) or Finder (Mac) under
   **OneDrive – University of South Florida**, and works like any other
   folder.

#### Rules

- Keep SHELF files in the shared folder, not on a personal drive, desktop, or external storage (e.g., a USB stick). Files saved elsewhere are
  invisible to the team and are not reliably backed up.
- Submit data by uploading to `01_Data/02_tables/staging/` and notifying
  the PI and the PI's lab manager (SOP 4, Section 4.4.1). Do not email data
  files.
- If you need to share a file without just pointing to it in the shared
  folder, send its OneDrive link, not an attachment, so that
  everyone works on one copy.
- Do not move, rename, or delete files or folders you did not create. Ask
  the PI's lab manager.
- Do not open the live database (`SHELF_DB_WORK.accdb`) unless you are on
  the database team. If you see a file with a computer name added to its
  name (a OneDrive conflict copy), tell the PI's lab manager rather than
  deleting it.
- To get back an older version of a file, right-click it in OneDrive on the
  web and choose **Version history**.

### 1.8.3 GitHub

GitHub is a website that stores files and keeps a full history of every
change: who made it, when, and why. Everything most team members need to do
on GitHub can be done in a web browser. For people who work with the
repository files often, there is also a desktop app, GitHub Desktop. SHELF
uses GitHub for the following repositories, both kept under the SHELF
organization on GitHub, `SHELF-USF` (<https://github.com/SHELF-USF>):

- **The SOPs** (repository `SHELF-SOP`, public). The Markdown files for
  SOPs 1–4 are kept here, and the SOP website and Word versions are built
  from them.
- **The database scripts** (repository `SHELF-DB`, private), described in SOP 4,
  Section 4.3.4.

Each repository has an **Issues** tab. An issue is a numbered note, visible to the whole team, about a problem
or a proposed change. Issues replace emails
and side conversations about the SOPs: the discussion, the decision, and the resulting change stay together and can be found later.

Issues can be given **labels** and **assigned** to people. Labels sort
issues by type and SOP, for example `SOP 2`, `error`, `unclear`,
`outdated`, `new step`, or `safety`. A maintainer assigns each issue to the person responsible for resolving it, and that person gets an email.

Each person has a role in the organization that sets what they can do.
Most team members have the **Triage** role: they can open, comment on,
label, and close issues, but cannot change files. Maintainers and the
database team have **Write** access. The PI and Co-PI are organization
owners.

#### Getting access

1. Create a free account at <https://github.com>. Using your USF email is
   recommended. Choose a username that identifies you.
2. Send your username to the PI's lab manager, who invites you to the
   `SHELF-USF` organization and its repositories.
3. Accept the invitation promptly, from the email GitHub sends you or from
   the bell icon at the top right of github.com. **Invitations expire after
   7 days.** If yours expires, ask the PI's lab manager to send a new one.
4. Optional: in the repository, click **Watch** and choose **Participating
   and @mentions** so that you get email when someone replies to you.

### 1.8.4 Suggesting a change to an SOP

These SOPs are living documents. They are revised as methods, equipment,
and personnel change, and the version number on the title page shows which release you are
reading (Section 1.7.1). Anyone on the team who finds an error,
something unclear, a step that no longer matches practice, or a missing
step should file an issue. Do not keep private notes or a marked-up copy:
if the SOP is wrong for you, it is likely wrong for others.

To file an issue:

1. Go to <https://github.com/SHELF-USF/SHELF-SOP> and sign in. (On the SOP
   website, the **Report an issue** link on each page does steps 1–3 for
   you.)
2. Click the **Issues** tab.
3. Click **New issue**.
4. Write a short title that names the SOP and section, e.g.,
   `SOP 3 §3.6.1: ethidium bromide volume missing`.
5. In the description, give:
   - the SOP and section number;
   - what is wrong, unclear, or missing;
   - the change you suggest, with replacement text if you have it;
   - why (for example, the protocol changed on a given date, or the
     current step fails in a specific way).
   You can paste photos or screenshots directly into the description.
6. Optional: on the right, add labels if you know which apply. Leave
   assignment to the maintainer.
7. Click **Create** (or **Submit new issue**).

**What happens next.** An SOP maintainer replies on the issue, usually
within two weeks. Questions and discussion happen in the issue's comments,
and GitHub emails you when someone replies. If the change is accepted, the
maintainer edits the SOP and closes the issue with a note saying which
version includes it. If it is not accepted, the issue is closed with the
reason. Either way, the record stays on GitHub.

For an urgent safety problem, tell the PI in person or by phone first, then
file an issue so the fix is recorded.

### 1.8.5 Maintaining the SOPs

The PI maintains SOPs 1, 2, and 4. The Co-PI and the Co-PI's lab manager
maintain SOP 3. Maintainers respond to issues and edit the SOP files in the `SHELF-SOP`
repository. The SOP website and Word version rebuild automatically when
changes are pushed. After each release, the PI's lab manager saves the
Word version to `09_SOP/`. For SOPs 2 and 3,
they also publish the revised text as a new version on protocols.io. Team
members who are comfortable with GitHub may propose edits directly as a
pull request. A maintainer reviews it before it is merged.

## 1.9 Glossary of shared terms

These terms are used the same way in all four SHELF SOPs.

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| CUFES | Continuous Underway Fish Egg Sampler (Checkley et al. 1997). It pumps near-surface seawater through a mesh sieve while the vessel is underway. |
| Station | A sampling location identified by its SEAMAP station label (e.g., `B111`). |
| Transect | One 30-minute CUFES collection. Transects are numbered in the order they are taken, starting from a station: `.1` is the transect that begins on station, `.2` is the next, and so on. A new transect is not started if the vessel will reach the next station in less than 35 minutes. |
| Sample | The contents of one transect, preserved in ethanol. A large sample may be split across multiple vials. |
| TransectID | The unique identifier for one transect: `[CruiseCode]_[Station].[N]`, where N is the transect number, for FIO cruises (e.g., `WB2410_B111.1`). The formats for other collection types are given in SOP 4, Section 4.2.2. |
| Contig | A consensus sequence assembled from overlapping DNA sequence reads. In SHELF, the reads from all eggs in a sample are assembled in Geneious (SOP 3, Section 3.8), and reads that overlap with ≥99% identity merge into one contig. Each egg is sequenced once, so one read is one egg. A contig therefore represents two or more eggs from the same transect that share a sequence, and one sample can have several contigs. Each contig is one record (`EggNumber = contigNN`; `NumberEggs` = number of reads in the contig). A read that does not assemble with any other read is a single egg, recorded with an integer `EggNumber`. |
| CruiseCode | The cruise identifier. FIO cruise codes are `[VesselCode][FY][NN]`, where FY is the Florida fiscal year (July–June) and NN is the cruise number within that year (e.g., `WB2410` is the 10th Weatherbird II cruise of FY2024). SEAMAP cruises use NOAA's cruise identifiers (e.g., `GU2403`, Gordon Gunter). See SOP 4, Section 4.2.2. |
| Fall time series | The founding long-term SHELF time series, built from fall (August–September) shelf collections on the SEAMAP Fall Plankton Survey and on FIO fall shelf cruises. Most SHELF samples belong to it. Its study component is `fall_shelf_time_series`. |
| Collection type | The program, habitat, and season of a cruise (e.g., `FIO_reef_spring`). The allowed values (a controlled vocabulary) are listed in SOP 4, Section 4.5. |
| Study component | The SHELF study a sample contributes to (e.g., `fall_shelf_time_series`). The allowed values (a controlled vocabulary) are listed in SOP 4, Section 4.5. |
| Controlled vocabulary | A fixed list of allowed values for a database field, so that every record uses the same terms and spelling. SHELF's controlled vocabularies are listed in SOP 4, Section 4.5. |
| Phase | A SHELF funding period, written with Roman numerals (SHELF Phase I, II, III, IV, V). The database stores phase as an integer (1–5). |
| FIO | Florida Institute of Oceanography |
| FLRACEP | Florida RESTORE Act Centers of Excellence Program |
| SEAMAP | Southeast Area Monitoring and Assessment Program (NOAA) |
| GRIIDC | Gulf of Mexico Research Initiative Information and Data Cooperative |
